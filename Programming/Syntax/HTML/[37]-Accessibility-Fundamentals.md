# Lesson 37 - Accessibility Fundamentals & Why It Matters

## 37.1 What Is Accessibility?

**Accessibility** (often abbreviated "a11y" — 11 letters between the "a" and "y") means designing and building web pages so people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with them. This includes people who are blind or low-vision, deaf or hard-of-hearing, have motor impairments, or have cognitive disabilities.

Much of what makes a page accessible is simply *using HTML correctly* — semantic elements, proper headings, labeled forms, and meaningful alt text, all covered in earlier lessons.

---

## 37.2 The POUR Principles

Accessibility guidelines (the WCAG — Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) are organized around four principles, often remembered as **POUR**:

- **Perceivable** — content must be presentable in ways users can perceive (e.g. alt text for images, captions for video).
- **Operable** — interface controls must be usable (e.g. everything reachable by keyboard, not just mouse).
- **Understandable** — content and interface behavior should be predictable and clear.
- **Robust** — content should work reliably across different browsers and assistive technologies.

---

## 37.3 Who Benefits?

Accessible design benefits far more people than it might initially seem:

- Screen reader users rely on semantic HTML to navigate.
- Users with motor impairments rely on full keyboard operability.
- Captions help deaf users *and* anyone watching video without sound.
- Clear structure and good contrast help users with cognitive disabilities *and* anyone using a phone in bright sunlight.

Accessibility isn't a separate feature bolted onto a finished page — it's largely a natural result of writing good, semantic HTML from the start, which is why it's woven throughout this entire course rather than treated as an afterthought.

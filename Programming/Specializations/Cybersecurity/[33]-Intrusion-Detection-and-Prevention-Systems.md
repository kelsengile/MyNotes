*Defensive Security*

# Lesson 33 - Intrusion Detection & Prevention Systems

## 33.1 IDS vs. IPS

An **IDS (Intrusion Detection System)** monitors network or system activity for signs of malicious behavior and generates alerts, but doesn't take direct action to block it. An **IPS (Intrusion Prevention System)** goes a step further, actively blocking or stopping detected malicious activity in real time. The trade-off is that an IPS's automated blocking, if triggered by a false positive, can disrupt legitimate traffic — making tuning especially important for IPS deployments.

---

## 33.2 Network-Based vs. Host-Based

- **NIDS/NIPS (Network-based)** — monitors traffic across a network segment, typically deployed at key points like the network perimeter, to detect attacks in transit.
- **HIDS/HIPS (Host-based)** — runs on an individual system, monitoring local activity like file changes, process behavior, and system calls, catching threats that never touch the network in an detectable way (e.g., malware delivered via USB).

Most mature environments deploy both, since each catches different classes of threats.

---

## 33.3 Detection Methods

- **Signature-based detection** — matches traffic or behavior against known attack patterns, similar in concept to antivirus signatures (Lesson 24). Effective against known threats, but blind to novel ones.
- **Anomaly-based detection** — establishes a baseline of "normal" behavior and flags significant deviations. Can catch unknown threats, but tends to generate more false positives, especially as legitimate usage patterns naturally change over time.

---

## 33.4 Where IDS/IPS Fits in the Bigger Picture

IDS/IPS complements, rather than replaces, the other controls covered earlier: firewalls (Lesson 9) restrict traffic based on rules, while IDS/IPS inspects the content and behavior of traffic that's already allowed through, looking for signs of an actual attack in progress. Alerts generated by an IDS/IPS typically feed into the SIEM and SOC workflow described in Lesson 32, and confirmed incidents move into the incident response process (Lesson 34).

---

## 33.5 Common Evasion Considerations

Attackers actively try to evade detection — through encryption (hiding payload content from signature matching), traffic fragmentation, or deliberately slow, low-volume activity designed to blend into normal traffic and avoid triggering anomaly thresholds. This is part of why defense in depth (Lesson 9) matters: no single detection layer catches everything, so overlapping controls are needed to catch what any individual layer misses.

# Lesson 45 - Security Policies & Frameworks (NIST, ISO 27001)

## 45.1 What is a Security Policy?

A **security policy** is a formal, documented statement of an organization's security expectations and rules — covering things like acceptable use of company systems, password requirements, data handling, and incident reporting obligations. Policies provide the authoritative "why" behind technical controls, and are often required for legal, regulatory, or contractual reasons, not just good practice.

Policies are typically supported by more detailed **standards** (specific, mandatory requirements, e.g., "passwords must be at least 12 characters"), **guidelines** (recommended but not mandatory best practices), and **procedures** (step-by-step instructions for carrying out a policy requirement).

---

## 45.2 The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework**, developed by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, is a widely adopted, voluntary framework for managing cybersecurity risk. It organizes security activities into core functions:

- **Govern** — establishing organizational context, risk strategy, and oversight.
- **Identify** — understanding assets, risks, and the organization's environment.
- **Protect** — implementing safeguards (many of the technical controls covered throughout this course).
- **Detect** — identifying security events as they occur (Lesson 32).
- **Respond** — taking action on detected incidents (Lesson 34).
- **Recover** — restoring capabilities and services after an incident.

The NIST CSF is deliberately flexible and outcome-focused rather than prescriptive, making it applicable across organizations of very different sizes and industries.

---

## 45.3 ISO/IEC 27001

**ISO/IEC 27001** is an international standard for establishing, implementing, and continually improving an **Information Security Management System (ISMS)** — a systematic, risk-based approach to managing an organization's information security. Unlike the NIST CSF, ISO 27001 is a certifiable standard: organizations can undergo a formal audit and receive certification demonstrating compliance, which is often used to build trust with customers and partners, particularly in international business contexts.

---

## 45.4 Other Notable Frameworks

- **CIS Controls** — a prioritized, practical set of defensive actions, complementing the CIS Benchmarks mentioned in Lesson 14.
- **COBIT** — a framework focused on IT governance and management more broadly, with strong ties to business objectives.
- **MITRE ATT&CK** — a knowledge base of real-world attacker tactics and techniques, widely used to structure detection engineering (Lesson 32) and threat modeling (Lesson 47) rather than as a governance framework per se.

---

## 45.5 Why Frameworks Matter

Frameworks provide a structured, proven starting point rather than requiring every organization to design a security program entirely from scratch. They also create a shared vocabulary for communicating security posture to leadership, auditors, regulators, and business partners — often a practical necessity, since compliance requirements (Lesson 46) frequently reference these same frameworks directly.

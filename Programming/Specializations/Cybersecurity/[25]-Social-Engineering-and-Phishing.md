# Lesson 25 - Social Engineering & Phishing

## 25.1 What is Social Engineering?

**Social engineering** is the manipulation of people, rather than technical systems, into performing an action or revealing information that compromises security. It exploits fundamentally human tendencies — trust, urgency, fear, helpfulness, and authority — which makes it effective regardless of how strong an organization's technical defenses are.

---

## 25.2 Common Social Engineering Techniques

- **Pretexting** — inventing a fabricated scenario (a "pretext") to gain trust, such as impersonating IT support to request a password reset.
- **Baiting** — offering something enticing (a free download, a labeled "Confidential" USB drive left in a parking lot) to lure a victim into a compromising action.
- **Tailgating/Piggybacking** — physically following an authorized person through a secure door without their own credentials.
- **Quid pro quo** — offering a service or benefit in exchange for information or access (e.g., a fake "IT survey" that asks for a password).
- **Authority and urgency** — impersonating an executive or creating a false sense of time pressure ("this must be done in the next 10 minutes") to short-circuit careful thinking.

---

## 25.3 Phishing and Its Variants

**Phishing** is the most common social engineering technique, typically delivered via email: a message designed to look legitimate, tricking the recipient into clicking a malicious link, opening an infected attachment, or entering credentials on a fake login page.

- **Spear phishing** — highly targeted phishing aimed at a specific individual, using personal details to appear more convincing.
- **Whaling** — spear phishing specifically targeting senior executives, often for high-value financial fraud.
- **Smishing** — phishing conducted via SMS text message.
- **Vishing** — phishing conducted via voice call, sometimes now enhanced with AI voice-cloning of a trusted person.
- **Business Email Compromise (BEC)** — impersonating (or actually compromising) an executive's email account to trick employees into fraudulent wire transfers.

---

## 25.4 Recognizing Phishing Attempts

Common red flags include: a mismatched or unusual sender address, urgency or threats ("your account will be closed"), generic greetings, unexpected attachments or links, requests for sensitive information a legitimate organization wouldn't ask for over email, and links that, when hovered over, point to a different domain than the text suggests.

---

## 25.5 Organizational Defenses

- **Security awareness training**, including simulated phishing exercises, to build a habit of skepticism.
- **Email filtering and anti-phishing technology** to catch known malicious patterns before they reach the inbox.
- **Verification procedures** for sensitive requests — e.g., always confirming a wire transfer request via a separate, known communication channel, never trusting the email alone.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)** (Lesson 36), which limits the damage even if credentials are successfully phished.
